#### Where Different RTC Supplier Types Have an Advantage?

It depends on the main bottleneck in the workload:

- If the bottleneck is real-time media quality: standalone RTC PaaS providers are stronger.
- If the bottleneck is integration with cloud data, AI, and security systems: integrated cloud platforms are stronger.
- If the bottleneck is phone numbers, carrier networks, and customer-journey orchestration: CPaaS providers are stronger.
- If the bottleneck is the unit cost of distributing content to a massive audience: CDNs are stronger.
- If the bottleneck is portability, private deployment, and customization of the media or AI pipeline: hosted open-source solutions are stronger.
- If the bottleneck is maximum control, specialized architecture, or long-run cost at very large and stable traffic volumes: in-house builds are stronger.

| Type | Workloads where it is strongest | Structural advantages | Relative weaknesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Standalone RTC PaaS | Global multi-party calling, social voice rooms, in-game voice chat, online classrooms, social entertainment, karaoke, co-hosted livestreaming, and in-app human-AI conversations | Built around resilience on poor networks, congestion control, routing, codecs, cross-device interoperability, and QoE diagnostics; cloud-neutral; deep scenario-specific SDKs | Usually weaker than CPaaS for PSTN, number compliance, and omnichannel workflows; more expensive than CDNs for pure scale-out viewing; vulnerable to bundled pricing from cloud platforms |
| Integrated cloud platform | Workloads that closely combine RTC with cloud databases, object storage, AI, content moderation, enterprise identity, and existing cloud contracts | Unified IAM, billing, data residency, and AI, storage, and security integrations; can package cloud discounts with existing customer relationships | RTC is often only one part of a broader product suite; investment in difficult network conditions, entertainment audio effects, heterogeneous endpoints, and cloud neutrality may lag specialized providers |
| CPaaS | Contact centers, notifications, IVR, marketing outreach, appointments, customer service, voice bots, and workflows combining phone, SMS, WhatsApp, email, and voice | Phone numbers, SIP/PSTN, carrier interconnection, numbering and messaging compliance, routing, identity, customer data, and omnichannel workflows | Large-group video, interactive livestreaming, complex media processing, and distribution to massive audiences are usually not core strengths |
| CDN | Sports events, product launches, e-commerce livestreams, financial news, and other broadcasts with one to a few hosts and tens of thousands to millions of viewers | Edge-node coverage, fan-out capacity, transcoding and adaptive bitrate, DVR/recording, and low cost per viewer | Weaker at two-way interaction, frequent speaker handoffs, complex room state, and uplink optimization under poor network conditions |
| Hosted open source | Developer-led conferencing, voice AI, multimodal agents, and use cases that require source visibility, private or hybrid deployment, or freedom to choose models | Open-source portability; media and agent pipelines can be modified, while global deployment, scaling, and observability are handled by the hosting provider | Once fully self-hosted, cross-region scheduling, TURN, Redis, upgrades, capacity planning, and 24/7 operations remain complex |
| In-house build | RTC is a core product differentiator, traffic is massive and stable, specialized hardware, protocols, or codecs are required, the network is closed, or data sovereignty requirements are exceptionally strict | Maximum control; topology can be optimized for a single business; at sufficient stable scale, supplier gross margin may be replaced by internal operating leverage | The fixed cost of a global network, poor-network algorithms, endpoint compatibility, fault diagnosis, and continuous evolution is extremely high; opportunity cost is the greatest |

**A phased view**

- Phase 1 (2020-2021): Zoom Video SDK, Azure Communication Services, Huawei Cloud SparkRTC, 100ms, Dyte, and LiveKit. The pandemic drove a surge in communications demand, including conference calls and online education.
- Phase 2 (2022-2023): Volcengine, StreamLake, Cloudflare, Dolby/Millicast, Mux, and AWS IVS. Use cases expanded into e-commerce livestreaming and interactive viewing.
- Phase 3 (2024 onward): No meaningful new entrants, while nearly all existing RTC providers expanded into voice AI. LiveKit has made the strongest strategic pivot.

#### Which RTC Providers Have Exited the Market Since 2020?

| Original provider / product | Nature of exit | Timing | Status after exit | Primary assessment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mux Real-Time Video | RTC product shut down entirely | January 2024 | Real-Time Video, Studio, and the Web Broadcast SDK were all discontinued; Mux retained on-demand and traditional livestreaming | The company did not disclose a reason. The most likely explanation is insufficient scale or expected returns in RTC, leading Mux to refocus on its core video businesses. |
| Twilio Live | Exit from interactive real-time livestreaming | November 2023 | APIs, documentation, and status pages were removed; Twilio retained the Video API | The company did not disclose a specific reason. It was likely a product-line contraction and consolidation of overlapping products under profitability pressure. |
| Vidyo.io | Exit from standalone RTC PaaS | July 2022 | The codebase is updated very infrequently, suggesting that the business is largely inactive | The product did not have enough business support to sustain the enterprise. |
| Dyte | Standalone supplier identity ended | 2025 | The team joined Cloudflare, and the product capabilities were folded into RealtimeKit; the underlying RTC technology continues to operate | The acquisition and team integration provided access to Cloudflare's global network and distribution. This was not a technology shutdown. |
| Selected CafeX WebRTC businesses | Business transfer and strategic pivot | After 2020 | Live Assist for Dynamics 365 was transferred to CBA; the product continued to be sold and supported by CBA, while CafeX shifted toward AI and enterprise data systems | CafeX no longer operated the general-purpose or vertical WebRTC business directly, reallocating resources to higher-level AI and data products. |
| 100ms | No exit from RTC, but the company signaled a shift toward healthcare AI | 2024 | No public evidence of an exit | The original business model became highly commoditized amid intense competition, encouraging a move toward higher-value verticals and use cases. |
